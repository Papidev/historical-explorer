# Basilica costantiniana di S. Agnese (Q124154237)

The **Mausoleum of Constantina**, also known as **Santa Costanza**, is a well-preserved **4th-century AD** building in **Rome**. It was originally built for **Constantina**, daughter of **Emperor Constantine I**, and later became a church. It stands on the **Via Nomentana**, part of the larger **Sant'Agnese fuori le mura** complex.

This mausoleum is a significant example of **Late Antiquity** architecture in **Rome**. It was built next to an earlier basilica, now in ruins, which **Constantine I** started in **338** as a funerary hall. The site also sits over part of the **Catacombs of Saint Agnes**, where the martyr **Saint Agnes** is believed to be buried.

Traditionally, the mausoleum was constructed during **Constantine I's** reign for his daughter **Constantina**, who died in **354**. Her sarcophagus was eventually housed here, though it might have been moved from an earlier location.

The mausoleum has a circular design with an ambulatory surrounding a central dome, largely retaining its original form. Despite some damage and past restorations, it remains an excellent example of **Early Christian art and architecture**. The vaults of the apses and ambulatory feature well-preserved **Late Roman mosaics**. While the central dome's mosaic is lost, **Francisco de Holanda** made watercolor copies between **1538** and **1540**, allowing for a hypothetical reconstruction. The large porphyry sarcophagus, believed to be either **Constantina's** or her sister **Helena's**, is now in the **Vatican Museum**.

### Location and Early History

**Santa Costanza** is located just off the **Via Nomentana**, outside **Rome's** ancient walls, in what was once a cemetery and possibly an Imperial family estate. The **Catacombs of Saint Agnes** were already present, containing the relics of **Saint Agnes**.

The adjacent funerary hall, or "**Constantinian basilica**," was begun in **338**, partly due to **Constantina's** devotion to **Saint Agnes**. Early Christians often sought burial near martyrs. The mausoleum itself was built around **350**. Excavations in **1992** suggest an earlier building, possibly a baptistry where **Constantina** and her paternal aunt were baptized by **Pope Silvester I** (**d. 335**), as recorded in **Liber Pontificalis**.

Attaching an important mausoleum to a basilica was common, similar to the **Mausoleum of Helena** (Constantine's mother) connected to the basilica of **Santi Marcellino e Pietro ad Duas Lauros** on **Via Labicana** (now **Via Casilina**). The bodies of **Constantina** and **Helena** were brought from **Bithynia** and **Gaul**, respectively, for burial here, as noted by **Ammianus**.

The **Constantinian basilica** eventually fell into ruin, but **Santa Costanza** survived almost intact. **Pope Nicholas I** celebrated mass here in **865**, the first recorded use of the name "**Santa Costanza**." It was officially consecrated as a church by **Pope Alexander IV** in **1254**, who moved **Constantina's** presumed remains under a central altar.

### Architecture and Design

The circular, centralized structure of **Santa Costanza** reflects its original purpose as a mausoleum for **Constantina** or **Helena**. This design, emphasizing the honored person or place, was typical for mausoleums and baptisteries of the period, seen also in **Split Cathedral** (Diocletian's mausoleum) and the **Rotunda of Galerius** in **Thessaloniki**.

The building features a high central space topped by a shallow dome, encircled by a darker ambulatory. Twelve pairs of granite columns with composite capitals support the dome's drum, separating the central area from the ambulatory. This design creates a contrast between the well-lit central space and the darker ambulatory. The original single entrance likely connected directly to the **Constantinian basilica**. The barrel-vaulted ambulatory, 22.5 meters (74 feet) in diameter, holds most of the surviving mosaics.

### Decoration

The mosaics in **Santa Costanza** are important examples of **Early Christian art**, including rare secular palace ceiling mosaics. While the central dome's mosaics are lost, those in the two apses and the ambulatory vault remain. The ambulatory vault mosaics, dating to the **4th century**, are contemporary with the building and show secular themes. They feature vine and plant ornaments, geometric patterns, birds, and vases. One notable panel depicts cupids picking grapes and making wine, recalling **Bacchus**, the Roman god of wine. This imagery led to the building being called "**Tempio di Bacco**" during the **Renaissance**. Such motifs reflect the transition between paganism and Christianity in **Rome**, or possibly construction under the non-Christian emperor **Julian the Apostate**.

The two apses in the ambulatory wall contain mosaics depicting **Christ as the Pantocrator**, among the earliest surviving examples. These likely date to the **5th or 7th century** and blend Roman imperial imagery with **Christian art**. One apse shows a **Traditio Legis**, with **Christ** giving **Saint Peter** a scroll inscribed "**DOMINUS PACEM DAT**" ("The Lord is giving Peace"). The second apse depicts **Christ** seated on a blue sphere, giving keys to **Saint Peter**, symbolizing divine authority granted to man and Roman authority sanctioned by God.

### Sarcophagi

Two large porphyry sarcophagi from the mausoleum are now in the **Vatican**. The more famous one, traditionally associated with **Constantina**, is in the **Vatican Museums** (moved in the **late 18th century**). The smaller one was moved to **St. Peter's Basilica** in **1606**. Current scholarship suggests the larger sarcophagus may have belonged to **Helena**, and the smaller to **Constantina**.

**Constantina's** sarcophagus (or the one attributed to her) features intricate relief designs of stylized vine-stems with cherubs, peacocks, and four portrait heads on the lid. This imagery, combining Dionysiac exuberance with symbols of immortality, reflects the blend of pagan and Christian themes common during this transitional period. Made of porphyry, a purple stone reserved for the imperial family and quarried exclusively from **Mons Porphyriticus** (**Coptos, Egypt**), it is a significant object for studying **Late Antiquity** art. A cast replica is placed in the mausoleum's ambulatory.

### Alternative Interpretations

Some scholars propose that the building was not originally intended as a church dedicated to **Santa Costanza**, as her veneration as a saint only emerged in the **16th century**. Excavations in **1987** and **1992** revealed a possible earlier triconch-shaped structure beneath, suggesting the current building might be a second Christian structure on the site, possibly built decades later for **Constantina's** sister **Helena** during the reign of **Julian the Apostate**. If true, the sarcophagi attributions would be reversed.
